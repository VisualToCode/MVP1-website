Why human-first technology beats every other tech strategy

Most technology firms hand over a product and disappear. Jay Pandya on why MVP1 was built deliberately the other way around, after 22 years watching the first model fail clients.

Jay Pandya17 November 202434 min

Most IT companies hand you a product and disappear.

Jay spent 22 years in corporate technology before starting MVP1 Ventures. The pattern he kept seeing was not incompetence. It was a delivery model where the engagement ends at handover, which means nobody is accountable for whether the thing actually worked commercially.

What human-first means in practice

It is not a statement about being nice to clients. It means the engagement starts with the people whose work the software changes, what they are trying to achieve, where the process actually breaks, rather than with a feature list. The technology decisions come after that, and they are judged against it.

Enterprise-grade, at SME scale

The other half of the argument is about who gets access to good engineering. Enterprise-grade architecture, security and delivery discipline have historically been priced for enterprises. Jay's position is that startups and SMEs need them more, not less, because they have less margin to absorb a failure.

Business models, not apps

The episode closes on the point that runs through all of Jay's work: the businesses that win the next decade will not be the ones that built the best app. They will be the ones that designed the better business model and used software to run it.

Key takeaways

  • The standard delivery model ends at handover, which is where commercial accountability should start.
  • Human-first means starting with the work the software changes, not the feature list.
  • Enterprise-grade discipline matters more for SMEs, not less.
  • Business model innovation beats app building.
